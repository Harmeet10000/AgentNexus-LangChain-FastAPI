## Why it is mandatory

Three reasons, in order of severity:

1. **Legal liability** — a model cannot bear responsibility for advice on a contract. A person must.
2. **Continuous improvement** — human corrections are the highest-quality training signal the system will ever get.
3. **Trust formation** — people trust tools they can correct, and correctly distrust tools they cannot.

In Indian legal work this is structural, not decorative: judgment interpretation, conflicting rulings across courts, and enforceability questions require context the system must surface for a qualified reviewer, not silently resolve.

## Where the gate sits

There are no cycles before human review — avoiding compounding hallucinations. The graph pipeline reaches `human_review` after risk analysis, compliance, and grounding verification have produced their findings, and before finalization or memory persistence. Execution pauses via LangGraph's `interrupt()`; the run sits in `awaiting_human_review`.

Interrupt types: `clarification_needed`, `plan_approval`, `ocr_reupload`, `human_review_required`.

## What humans do

| Action | Mechanism |
| --- | --- |
| Approve or reject risks | `resume` frame with `action: "approve" | "reject"` |
| Correct clauses | `modified_plan` / override entries in the resume payload |
| Annotate reasoning | free-text `feedback` (up to 8192 chars) |
| Take responsibility | `reviewer_role` recorded alongside the decision |

## What gets stored

Overrides, comments, and reviewer role persist as first-class data attached to the run's state — becoming training data and audit data, not just UI feedback. Persisted memory is *trusted* memory precisely because only reviewed output crosses the gate.

## Audit trail

Because decisions are state, they replay: who decided what, when, on which document version, with which annotation. That satisfies compliance review and gives future runs defensible provenance.
